This project introduces two foundational business concepts — Systems Thinking and Economies of Scale — through a structured factory production scenario comparing small-scale and large-scale operations.

The core task was to apply systems thinking analysis to a manufacturing system, then model the economic impact of scaling production volume. By holding fixed costs constant and varying output, the assignment demonstrated how unit costs can fall dramatically when production is scaled up.

This concept matters because it explains one of the most powerful forces in industrial economics: why large manufacturers can produce at structurally lower costs than small ones, and how this advantage shapes trade competitiveness, market structure, and corporate strategy.

A — What Is a System
A system is a group of connected things that work together. It has inputs (what enters), a process (the transformation), and outputs (what results). Inside the system, stocks accumulate and flows change them over time.
⬇

Input

Resources or energy entering — raw materials, sunlight, capital, labour.

⚙

Process

The transformation mechanism — photosynthesis, manufacturing, digestion.

⬆

Output

What emerges — oxygen, products, profit, heat, or waste.

📦

Stock

What accumulates — water in a tank, inventory in a warehouse, energy in leaves.

🔄

Flow

Rate of change in stock — litres per hour, units per day, kg per season.

↩

Feedback

Outputs loop back and alter future inputs — self-regulation or amplification.

B — Systems Thinking Method
Systems thinking is the practice of studying the relationships between parts of a system — not just the individual parts. It asks: how do pieces interact, reinforce, or constrain each other to produce emergent behaviour?
1

Define Boundaries

What is inside vs. outside? Where does the system begin and end? What forces are external vs. internal?

2

Identify the Parts

List all components, actors, and elements existing within your defined boundary.

3

Identify the Relationships

Map the connections — which parts affect each other, in which direction, and how strongly?

4

Identify Flows and Stock

What accumulates (stock)? What changes those quantities over time (flows)? What rate?

5

Identify the Function

What is the system's overall purpose? What behaviour does it produce as a whole?

F — Limits to Growth
Can economies of scale continue forever?
No. Every system has boundaries. Beyond a certain scale, costs stop falling and begin rising — called diseconomies of scale. Four categories of external forces also act to cap or disrupt growth, no matter how efficient the internal system becomes.
📉

Decreased Demand

Markets have finite size. Producing beyond what demand can absorb forces price cuts and inventory build-up, eroding the very margins that made scale attractive.

⚔

Competition

Profitable high-volume production attracts rivals. Competitors undercut price or out-innovate, shrinking the margin benefit of scale.

📜

Regulations

Governments impose labour laws, environmental limits, antitrust rules, and tariffs — each adds cost or caps production volumes directly.

🔬

Technological Disruption

A better process, material, or product can make an existing scaled system obsolete — scale advantage disappears when the technology changes.

🏗

Management Complexity

Large organisations face rising coordination costs, slower communication, and bureaucracy — these are diseconomies of scale that raise unit cost from the inside.

🌍

Resource Constraints

Materials, energy, skilled labour, and land are finite. Scarcity raises input costs at very high production volumes, reversing the cost curve.

Evidence 4 — Formula Reference Sheet Lecture Formula Card
Total Output Q = M × U  (machines × units per machine per day)
Variable Cost TVC = Q × v  (total output × variable cost per unit)
Total Daily Cost TC = FC + TVC  (fixed cost stays constant regardless of output)
Cost per Unit CPU = TC ÷ Q  → falls as Q rises (this is economies of scale)
Daily Revenue R = p × Q  (sale price × total output)
Daily Profit π = R − TC  (revenue minus total cost)
Margin per Unit m = p − CPU  (positive only when price exceeds cost per unit)
Break-even Price p* = CPU  (minimum price to cover all costs — varies with scale)
04 · Reflection

What Did I Learn?

"The most surprising thing was not that larger scale is cheaper — it's how dramatically the cost curve bends. A 5× increase in machines produced an 80% drop in unit cost."
🧠
Dimension 1
Knowledge
  • Systems are defined by their relationships, not just their components — a factory is the loop between materials, labour, and profit, not merely the machines.
  • Fixed costs are the engine of economies of scale. They distribute across more units as volume grows without changing in absolute value.
  • The break-even price equals cost per unit — and it changes every time production volume changes, not just when prices do.
  • Feedback loops determine system behaviour: the factory's profit-to-materials loop is a reinforcing (growth) loop when profitable.
  • Diseconomies of scale are internal failure modes — coordination costs, bureaucracy, and resource scarcity that reverse the cost curve at very large scale.
⚙
Dimension 2
Skills
  • Cost modelling: applying the CPU formula step by step to compute total cost, unit cost, revenue, and profit margin from raw inputs.
  • Systems mapping: identifying boundaries, components, stocks, flows, and feedback loops in an industrial context.
  • Comparative scenario analysis: holding some variables fixed (cost, price, workers) while changing others (machines) to isolate the causal mechanism.
  • Data visualisation: translating raw numbers into bar charts, comparison tables, and interactive tools to make patterns visible.
  • Critical analysis: questioning assumptions — recognising that scale advantages are real but bounded by demand, regulation, and management limits.
💭
Dimension 3
Reflection

What was difficult: Initially I assumed all costs would rise with production volume. Understanding that fixed costs stay absolutely constant — and that this distinction is the entire mechanism behind economies of scale — required working through several examples before it clicked.

What surprised me: The mathematical impact was non-linear. A 5× increase in machines reduced cost per unit by 80%, turning a zero-profit operation into one earning ₩8 per unit. The same fixed cost behaves very differently at different volumes.

What I would explore further: How diseconomies emerge in practice — at what exact scale do management costs start rising faster than the fixed-cost savings? This seems central to understanding why no firm scales infinitely.

🌐
Real-World Connection — International Trade
Scale economics explains why large industrial exporters — Samsung in electronics, Hyundai in automobiles, POSCO in steel — hold structural cost advantages that smaller competitors cannot close through price competition alone. When a Korean manufacturer produces five million units versus a rival's one hundred thousand, the fixed-cost advantage is mathematically insurmountable. In international trade theory, this connects directly to comparative advantage: a country's production cost structure, shaped in part by its capacity to achieve scale, determines where it can produce most efficiently and therefore what it should export. The factory scenario in this assignment is a microcosm of that larger dynamic.

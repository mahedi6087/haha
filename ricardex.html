Project Overview
🌐
What Is This Page About?
Assignment context · Core concept · Why it matters

This project was built as part of an International Trade Economics course assignment exploring the foundations of why nations trade. The assignment asked us to analyze two countries producing two goods and determine, step by step, who holds absolute advantage, how to construct an opportunity cost matrix, and which country should specialize in which good according to Comparative Advantage — David Ricardo's central insight from 1817.

Rather than submitting a static worksheet, I built an interactive tool that replicates the full analytical workflow. Any two countries and any two goods can be entered, and the tool computes every result instantly — from raw productivity comparisons through to PPF equations and world output gains from specialization.

Course Assignment
International Trade Economics — Comparative Advantage Analysis
⚡
Absolute Advantage
A country has absolute advantage when it can produce more of a good per resource unit than another. Introduced by Adam Smith. Important, but not the full story.
⚖️
Opportunity Cost
The real cost of producing one unit of a good is the quantity of the other good that must be given up. Calculated as: OC(Good 1) = output(Good 2) ÷ output(Good 1).
🎯
Comparative Advantage
Ricardo's breakthrough: a country should specialize in the good where its opportunity cost is lowest, even if it is worse at producing everything. This is why trade benefits all nations.
📈
Production Possibility Frontier
The PPF shows the maximum combinations of two goods a country can produce with fixed resources. Its slope equals the opportunity cost — a direct visual of trade-offs.
💡
Why this matters in the real world: Comparative advantage explains patterns in global trade — why Bangladesh exports garments, why Germany exports machinery, and why even a highly productive country gains from importing goods other nations produce at lower opportunity cost. It underpins the WTO, free trade agreements, and development economics debates about export-led growth.

The following evidence reproduces the core calculations and visual outputs from the assignment worksheet, using the in-class example of Uzmara (Country A) vs Fleuronne (Country B) producing Storm-Iron and Sungrain.

🔢
Step-by-Step Opportunity Cost Calculation
01
Given data:
Uzmara: Storm-Iron = 12, Sungrain = 6, Resources = 100
Fleuronne: Storm-Iron = 8, Sungrain = 2, Resources = 100
02
Absolute Advantage check:
Storm-Iron: Uzmara 12 > Fleuronne 8 → Uzmara wins
Sungrain: Uzmara 6 > Fleuronne 2 → Uzmara wins
Both goods → Uzmara has absolute advantage in both.
03
Opportunity Cost — Uzmara:
OC(Storm-Iron) = 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5 Sungrain
OC(Sungrain) = 12 ÷ 6 = 2.0 Storm-Iron
04
Opportunity Cost — Fleuronne:
OC(Storm-Iron) = 2 ÷ 8 = 0.25 Sungrain
OC(Sungrain) = 8 ÷ 2 = 4.0 Storm-Iron
05
Comparative Advantage:
Storm-Iron OC: Fleuronne 0.25 < Uzmara 0.5 → Fleuronne specializes
Sungrain OC: Uzmara 2.0 < Fleuronne 4.0 → Uzmara specializes

What Did I Learn?
🧩
Reflection Across Three Dimensions
Knowledge · Skills · Reflection
📚 Knowledge
🔬
Concepts I Learned
  • Absolute vs comparative advantage are fundamentally different. A country can be worse at producing everything and still have a comparative advantage — because advantage is about relative efficiency, not absolute output.
  • Opportunity cost is the correct measure for trade decisions, not raw productivity. The OC formula (Good B ÷ Good A) translates productivity ratios into trade-off ratios.
  • The PPF's slope equals the opportunity cost of the good on the x-axis. This gave me an intuitive visual anchor for comparing OC values across countries.
  • Specialization increases total world output, even when one country holds absolute advantage in both goods. Ricardo proved this mathematically and I verified it through the simulator.
🛠 Skills
⚙️
Analytical & Technical Skills Built
  • Quantitative analysis: Setting up and computing a 2×2 opportunity cost matrix from raw productivity data — a skill that generalizes to any resource allocation comparison.
  • Data interpretation: Reading a comparative advantage result correctly — lower OC cell = specialization recommendation.
  • Critical thinking: Questioning why the country with absolute advantage in both goods still benefits from specializing in just one. The answer required understanding the concept of scarcity of resources.
  • Visualization: Translating a linear production equation into PPF coordinates — plotting intercepts and interpreting the slope as an economic trade-off ratio.
  • Decision modeling: Using the "what-if" simulator to test how world output changes across different resource allocation scenarios, building intuition beyond the textbook case.
💭 Reflection
🌍
What Was Difficult, What Surprised Me, Real-World Applications

What was difficult: The hardest part was accepting that absolute advantage is irrelevant to the specialization decision. My initial instinct was "the better country should produce everything." Working through the opportunity cost matrix numerically — and seeing that Fleuronne's OC of Storm-Iron (0.25) is half of Uzmara's (0.5) even though Uzmara produces more of it — is what finally made Ricardo's argument concrete.

What surprised me: The gains from trade do not always mean every country produces more of every good. Uzmara produces less Storm-Iron under full specialization than in the no-trade baseline. What improves is world Sungrain — the country with the comparative advantage concentrates fully there. This subtlety is often lost in high-level summaries of the theory.

Real-world applications: Bangladesh exports ready-made garments — not because it has the world's highest garment productivity, but because the opportunity cost of garment production relative to alternatives is lower than in richer countries. Similarly, Singapore trades financial services and technology rather than food, even though food production would be physically possible. The model also explains why trade restrictions can harm both parties: tariffs and quotas force countries to produce outside their comparative advantage, reducing global output. This directly applies to Bangladesh's own import tariff policies — restricting imports of goods other countries produce at lower OC creates production inefficiency at home.

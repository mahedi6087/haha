01 · Project Overview

The Picking Problem

Order picking accounts for up to 55% of warehouse operating costs. Finding a fast, repeatable picking route is a real-world optimization challenge.

📦

The Scenario

You are a warehouse picker with 12 items to collect. Each item is in a fixed shelf location. Start and end at the depot. Minimize total travel distance.

🗺️

The Warehouse

6 parallel aisles with shelves on each side. Items are scattered across all aisles and shelf positions, reflecting a realistic storage layout.

⚡

Why Heuristics?

The exact solution (TSP) is NP-hard. Heuristics give near-optimal routes in seconds — essential when routes must be regenerated per order, hundreds of times daily.

📐

Three Methods

Zone-Based Picking groups items by area. S-Shape sweeps aisles in sequence. Largest Gap avoids unnecessary aisle penetration using a mid-point return rule.

Three Picking Heuristics

Heuristic 1
Zone-Based Picking

Divide the warehouse into vertical zones (groups of aisles). Collect all items in Zone 1 before moving to Zone 2, and so on. Within each zone, pick items in the order they appear moving inward.

1Assign each item to a zone based on its aisle column.
2Sort zones from nearest to farthest (left → right).
3Within each zone, enter the aisle and collect all zone items before exiting.
4Return to depot after clearing all zones.

Heuristic 2
S-Shape (Serpentine)

Traverse every aisle that contains at least one item in a snake-like pattern. Enter from one end and exit from the other, alternating direction each aisle. Never backtrack within an aisle.

1Identify which aisles contain items.
2Enter aisle 1 from the bottom, sweep to top collecting items.
3Cross over to aisle 2, sweep back down. Alternate.
4After last item aisle, travel back to depot.

Heuristic 3
Largest Gap

For each aisle with items, find the largest gap between adjacent items (including depot and aisle end). Enter from the same end as the first item, return from the other side — unless the gap at the top is larger, in which case turn around at that gap.

1Sort items within each aisle by shelf position.
2Identify the largest gap: from depot-end, between any two items, or at the top.
3If largest gap is at the top: enter, collect all items, exit same side (return).
4Otherwise: enter from nearest end, collect up to gap, exit at gap (traverse).

05 · What Did I Learn?

Reflections

Three dimensions of learning from designing and comparing warehouse picking heuristics.

Knowledge

Heuristics vs Exact Methods

I learned that a heuristic doesn't guarantee the optimal solution but finds a good-enough answer quickly. The Largest Gap method — while not mathematically proven to be optimal — consistently outperforms S-Shape and Zone-Based on this layout because it explicitly avoids unnecessary travel inside aisles. The key insight: reducing within-aisle travel is more impactful than reducing aisle-to-aisle travel.

Skills

Route Modeling & Spatial Reasoning

Translating a physical warehouse into a 2D coordinate grid — then computing Manhattan distances along allowed paths — taught me how to model real-world constraints in an algorithm. I practiced decomposing a large problem (12-item route) into smaller sub-problems (per-aisle decisions), which is a core skill in operations research and any multi-step optimization task.

Reflection

Surprises & Industry Context

What surprised me most: the S-Shape heuristic — the simplest and most widely used in real warehouses — was the worst performer on this layout. That's because it blindly traverses full aisles regardless of item density. In sparse aisles, this wastes significant time. Real warehouses use Largest Gap or combined return/traversal strategies for exactly this reason. The gap between "easy to implement" and "best performance" is a real design trade-off.
